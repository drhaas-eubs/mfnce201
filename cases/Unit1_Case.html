Teaching case
A trade is only a promise until settlement; markets are worth what their plumbing guarantees, not what their screens display.Core message · Unit 1 · ILO 2, 3

Isar Precision Tools GmbH is a family-owned maker of high-precision cutting tools based in Munich. It employs 1,400 people and earns €620 million in revenue, 45% of it from customers in the United States who pay in US dollars. Most of its costs — wages, steel, energy — are in euros. For two decades the company financed itself through its house banks. That is changing: the family wants to fund a new plant in Ohio and to give the next generation an exit route.

The CFO, Katharina Brandl, has three options on the table. The first is a €50 million, seven-year bond listed on a regulated market in Frankfurt. The second is a small initial public offering of 25% of the shares. The third is to keep borrowing from the banks and do nothing about capital markets for now. The supervisory board wants a recommendation in four weeks.

Brandl knows the bond market better than the equity market. A bond means a fixed coupon, covenants and a rating process. An IPO means a prospectus, a stock exchange listing, quarterly reporting and shareholders who will trade the stock every day. In either case the securities would be held in a central securities depository and trades would be cleared through a central counterparty. Her treasury team cannot explain to the board who exactly carries the risk between the moment an investor buys and the moment the investor owns.

The timing adds pressure. On 11 October 2027 the EU settlement cycle shortens from two business days to one. Brokers have warned that confirmations, allocations and the related FX trades for non-euro investors will need to be finished on trade date. Several US funds that would buy Isar's bond would need to buy euros to do so.

The dollar problem is older. Each month Isar receives around $23 million from US customers. When the euro strengthened by 8% against the dollar in one recent year, operating margin fell by almost two percentage points. The company has never hedged. The CEO, Peter Isarwald, calls hedging 'speculation with extra steps'. Brandl calls the unhedged position 'speculation without a seatbelt'. The new Ohio plant would earn and spend dollars, which changes the exposure again.

Brandl has asked a team of Master in Finance interns to prepare the analysis. She wants to understand the infrastructure behind each financing route, to see what the interest-rate differential between the euro area and the United States implies for a 12-month forward rate, and to know which of Isar's exposures can be hedged with financial instruments and which only by changing the business.

Your tasks

  1. Map the trade lifecycle for one investor buying Isar's bond: which institutions are involved at each stage and what risk does each one remove?
  2. Using current policy rates, calculate an indicative 12-month EUR/USD forward rate with covered interest rate parity. What does it imply for Isar's dollar receipts?
  3. Classify Isar's currency exposures as transaction, translation or economic. Which can be hedged with forwards or options, and which only operationally?
  4. How does the Ohio plant change the exposure and the case for hedging?
  5. Decision: recommend one financing route and one hedging policy to the supervisory board, with the two strongest arguments for each.
