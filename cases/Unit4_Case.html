Teaching case
Transparency is not accountability: a blockchain can show every transaction and still leave no one answerable when things go wrong.Core message · Unit 4 · ILO 3, 4, 5

Atlântico Pay is a licensed electronic money institution based in Lisbon. Its customers are mainly Brazilian citizens living in Portugal and Spain who send money home each month. Today Atlântico routes these remittances through two correspondent banks. A typical €300 transfer takes one to two business days to arrive and costs the sender around 4.5% in fees and exchange-rate margin.

The CEO, Rita Monteiro, wants to cut cost and time sharply. Her plan is to issue a euro e-money token, 'EURA', on a public proof-of-stake blockchain. Senders would buy EURA in the Atlântico app. A partner exchange in São Paulo would convert EURA into Brazilian reais and pay out to the recipient's bank account through Brazil's instant payment system. Atlântico estimates that total cost would fall to around 1.2% and that most transfers would arrive within minutes.

Under the EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, a token referencing a single official currency is an e-money token. It may only be issued by a credit institution or an electronic money institution, must be redeemable at par at any time, and the reserves backing it must be held in secure, low-risk assets. Atlântico would need to notify its supervisor and publish a crypto-asset white paper. Its supervisor has called a hearing before deciding whether to raise objections and which conditions to impose.

Several stakeholders have asked to be heard. A consumer association supports lower fees but worries about older customers losing access to their tokens if they lose their phones or keys. A large incumbent bank argues that a payment company should not compete with banks without the same capital requirements. Economists at the central bank ask what would happen to monetary policy transmission if a large share of payments moved to tokens held outside the banking system, and how the Brazilian side would view a euro token circulating in its economy.

There are also operational concerns. An external auditor has found that Atlântico's private keys for the token contract are held by two employees, with no hardware security module and no tested recovery plan. A sustainability analyst notes that proof-of-stake consumes far less energy than proof-of-work, but asks how Atlântico will report the environmental impact required under MiCA. Atlântico's own treasury team points out that most of the remaining 1.2% cost is the BRL conversion in São Paulo, which the blockchain does not remove.

The hearing must answer a single question: should EURA be allowed to launch, and if so, on what conditions?

Your tasks

  1. Classify EURA under MiCA. Which issuer obligations follow, and which risks do they address?
  2. Assess the efficiency claim: which parts of the 4.5% does blockchain remove, and which remain? Link your answer to the FX concepts from Unit 1.
  3. Apply the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 to the auditor's finding. Which functions are weakest, and what would you require?
  4. Using Lessig's four modalities and the Triple Bottom Line, identify the main ethical and social implications of launching EURA.
  5. Decision: as the supervisor, approve, approve with conditions or refuse. State your three most important conditions or reasons.

Composite teaching case. Atlântico Pay, EURA and all figures are invented. MiCA (Regulation (EU) 2023/1114) is real; its rules on e-money tokens have applied since 30 June 2024.
