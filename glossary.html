Unit 1 · Securities, Markets and Foreign Exchange

Central counterparty (CCP)Unit 1

An entity that interposes itself between counterparties to contracts traded in one or more financial markets, becoming the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer.

CPSS-IOSCO (2012) Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures. Basel: BIS.
Central securities depository (CSD)Unit 1

An entity that provides securities accounts, central safekeeping services and asset services, and plays a key role in ensuring the integrity of securities issues.

CPSS-IOSCO (2012) Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures. Basel: BIS.
Delivery versus payment (DvP)Unit 1

A securities settlement mechanism that links a securities transfer and a funds transfer so that delivery occurs if and only if the corresponding payment occurs.

BIS (1992) Delivery versus Payment in Securities Settlement Systems. Basel: BIS.
Settlement cycle (T+n)Unit 1

The number of business days between the trade date (T) and the date on which securities and cash are finally exchanged.

Regulation (EU) 2025/2075 amending Regulation (EU) No 909/2014. Official Journal of the European Union.
Bid-ask spreadUnit 1

The difference between the highest price a buyer is willing to pay and the lowest price a seller is willing to accept; a measure of transaction cost and liquidity.

Harris, L. (2003) Trading and Exchanges: Market Microstructure for Practitioners. Oxford: Oxford University Press.
Forward exchange rateUnit 1

An exchange rate agreed today for the delivery of currencies at a specified future date; under covered interest parity it reflects the spot rate adjusted for the interest-rate differential.

Eiteman, D.K., Stonehill, A.I. and Moffett, M.H. (2016) Multinational Business Finance. 14th edn. Boston: Pearson.
Transaction exposureUnit 1

The sensitivity of a firm's contracted future foreign-currency cash flows to changes in exchange rates between the contract date and the settlement date.

Eiteman, D.K., Stonehill, A.I. and Moffett, M.H. (2016) Multinational Business Finance. 14th edn. Boston: Pearson.

Unit 2 · Introduction to Blockchain Technology

Distributed ledger technology (DLT)Unit 2

A technology that enables the operation and use of a ledger that is shared, replicated and synchronised among several network nodes, of which blockchain is one form.

Regulation (EU) 2022/858 on a pilot regime for market infrastructures based on distributed ledger technology. Official Journal of the European Union.
Hash functionUnit 2

A mathematical function that maps data of any size to a fixed-length output, such that a small change in input produces a completely different output and the input cannot feasibly be reconstructed.

Drescher, D. (2017) Blockchain Basics. New York: Apress.
Consensus mechanismUnit 2

The set of rules and procedures by which the nodes of a distributed ledger agree on the validity and order of transactions.

Bano, S. et al. (2019) 'SoK: Consensus in the age of blockchains', Proceedings of the 1st ACM Conference on Advances in Financial Technologies, pp. 183–198.
Private keyUnit 2

A secret number that allows its holder to sign transactions and thereby control the assets associated with the corresponding public key or address.

Diffie, W. and Hellman, M.E. (1976) 'New directions in cryptography', IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, 22(6), pp. 644–654.
Smart contractUnit 2

A computerised transaction protocol that executes the terms of a contract automatically when predefined conditions are met.

Szabo, N. (1997) 'Formalizing and securing relationships on public networks', First Monday, 2(9).
Permissioned blockchainUnit 2

A blockchain in which only approved participants may validate transactions and, often, read the ledger; typical of consortia of financial institutions.

Drescher, D. (2017) Blockchain Basics. New York: Apress.
OracleUnit 2

A service that supplies external, off-chain data to a smart contract, thereby reintroducing a trusted source of information.

Caldarelli, G. (2020) 'Understanding the blockchain oracle problem: a call for action', Information, 11(11), 509.

Unit 3 · Application of Blockchain Technology in Finance

TokenisationUnit 3

The process of representing rights to an asset as digital tokens on a distributed ledger.

OECD (2020) The Tokenisation of Assets and Potential Implications for Financial Markets. Paris: OECD.
Security tokenUnit 3

A token that represents a financial instrument, such as a share or bond, and is therefore subject to securities regulation.

FINMA (2018) Guidelines for Enquiries Regarding the Regulatory Framework for ICOs. Bern: FINMA.
DLT Pilot RegimeUnit 3

An EU regulatory regime that allows authorised market infrastructures to trade and settle tokenised financial instruments on distributed ledgers under temporary exemptions from certain requirements.

Regulation (EU) 2022/858. Official Journal of the European Union, L 151.
StablecoinUnit 3

A crypto-asset designed to maintain a stable value relative to a reference asset, typically a fiat currency, through reserves or algorithmic mechanisms.

Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 on markets in crypto-assets (MiCA). Official Journal of the European Union, L 150.
Automated market maker (AMM)Unit 3

A smart-contract-based exchange that prices trades using a mathematical formula applied to pooled reserves rather than matching buy and sell orders.

Adams, H., Zinsmeister, N. and Robinson, D. (2020) Uniswap v2 Core.
Decentralised finance (DeFi)Unit 3

Financial services such as exchange, lending and derivatives provided through smart contracts on public blockchains without traditional intermediaries.

Schär, F. (2021) Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis Review, 103(2), pp. 153–174.
Wholesale CBDCUnit 3

A digital form of central bank money accessible to financial institutions for settling interbank and securities transactions.

BIS Innovation Hub, SNB and SIX (2020) Project Helvetia. Basel: BIS.

Unit 4 · Potential Implications

E-money token (EMT)Unit 4

A crypto-asset that purports to maintain a stable value by referencing the value of one official currency.

Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 (MiCA), Article 3. Official Journal of the European Union, L 150.
Asset-referenced token (ART)Unit 4

A crypto-asset that is not an e-money token and that purports to maintain a stable value by referencing another value or right, or a combination thereof, including one or more official currencies.

Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 (MiCA), Article 3. Official Journal of the European Union, L 150.
Crypto-asset service provider (CASP)Unit 4

A legal person or undertaking authorised under MiCA to provide crypto-asset services, such as custody, exchange or operation of a trading platform, to clients.

Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 (MiCA). Official Journal of the European Union, L 150.
Regulatory arbitrageUnit 4

Structuring an activity to take advantage of differences or gaps between regulatory regimes so as to avoid stricter requirements.

Financial Stability Board (2023) High-level Recommendations for the Regulation, Supervision and Oversight of Crypto-asset Activities and Markets. Basel: FSB.
Front-running (MEV)Unit 4

Profiting from advance knowledge of pending transactions by placing one's own transaction ahead of them; on public blockchains this is captured as maximal extractable value.

Daian, P. et al. (2020) 'Flash Boys 2.0: frontrunning in decentralized exchanges, miner extractable value, and consensus instability', 2020 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy, pp. 910–927.
Monetary sovereigntyUnit 4

A state's ability to issue its own currency and conduct an independent monetary policy, which can be weakened when residents widely use money issued abroad or by private entities.

Bank for International Settlements (2023) Annual Economic Report 2023. Basel: BIS.
