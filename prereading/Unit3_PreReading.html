What a token is — and is not

A token is an entry on a distributed ledger that represents a right. It can represent a currency unit, access to a service, a share in a company, a unit in a fund or a fraction of a building. What it represents is not decided by the technology but by law and contract. A token that the issuer calls a 'utility token' may be a security in the eyes of a supervisor; a token that claims to represent a building may give the holder no enforceable right to it at all.

Supervisors therefore classify tokens by their economic function. The Swiss regulator FINMA distinguished payment, utility and asset tokens in 2018. In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission applies the Howey test from 1946: an investment of money in a common enterprise with an expectation of profit from the efforts of others is a security, whatever it is called. In the EU, tokenised financial instruments fall under existing securities law such as MiFID II, while other crypto-assets are covered by the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA).

Where the gains come from

The OECD described tokenisation as a lifecycle: origination of the asset, legal structuring, issuance, trading, servicing and redemption. Gains are possible at each stage. Issuance can be cheaper when fewer intermediaries are involved. Smaller denominations open assets to more investors. Servicing — paying coupons, rents or dividends, processing corporate actions — can be automated in smart contracts. Trading and settlement can be compressed into a single step.

This last point connects to Unit 1. On today's markets, trading and settlement are separated by a gap of one or two days, filled by clearinghouses and depositories. On a shared ledger, the security token and the payment can, in principle, move in one indivisible transaction: atomic settlement, the purest form of delivery versus payment. Project Helvetia, run by the Swiss National Bank, SIX and the BIS Innovation Hub in 2020, showed that tokenised securities could settle in central bank money in this way.

The phrase 'in principle' matters. Atomic settlement requires the cash leg to be on, or linked to, the same ledger. If the token settles instantly but the money moves through the traditional payment system, the gap returns. The choice of cash leg — stablecoin, tokenised bank deposit or central bank money — is therefore one of the central design decisions of any tokenised market. Instant settlement also removes the netting that CCPs provide, so participants must pre-fund every trade.

Compliance in the token

Regulated securities cannot be sold to anyone, anywhere. Investors must be identified, some products may only be sold to professionals, and transfers to sanctioned persons are forbidden. On a traditional market these rules are enforced by brokers and custodians. On a ledger they can also be written into the token itself: a smart contract can refuse a transfer to an address that is not on a list of verified investors, freeze tokens on a court order or limit how many tokens a retail investor may hold.

Raphael Auer at the Bank for International Settlements took the idea further with 'embedded supervision'. If a market runs on a shared ledger, the supervisor could monitor compliance by reading the ledger directly rather than waiting for firms to send reports. This could lower reporting costs, especially for small firms, and allow problems to be seen sooner. It also raises hard questions about privacy, data protection and the reliability of the ledger itself.

In the EU, the DLT Pilot Regime has applied since March 2023 and allows authorised market infrastructures to trade and settle tokenised financial instruments on distributed ledgers under temporary exemptions. It is a sandbox at the scale of a continent: regulators learn what works before deciding on permanent rules.

DeFi: finance without intermediaries, but not without risk

Decentralised finance takes tokenisation further. Fabian Schär describes DeFi as a stack: a blockchain for settlement, tokens as assets, protocols for exchange and lending, applications that users interact with, and aggregators that combine them. Automated market makers such as Uniswap replace order books with liquidity pools priced by a formula. Lending protocols replace credit analysis with overcollateralisation and automatic liquidation. Perpetual futures, an idea Robert Shiller proposed in 1993, have become the most traded crypto derivative.

These mechanisms are transparent and run continuously, but their risks are new. Smart-contract bugs, manipulated price oracles and cascading liquidations have caused large losses. Because protocols build on each other, a failure in one layer travels to others.

Payments and foreign exchange

Cross-border payments are slow and expensive because they pass through chains of correspondent banks across time zones. Blockchain-based solutions aim to shorten the chain: stablecoins for retail transfers, and for wholesale payments, shared platforms on which several central banks issue digital currencies, as tested in Project mBridge. Such platforms can achieve payment-versus-payment settlement of currency trades, removing the risk that one leg is paid and the other is not. They also raise questions about governance and monetary sovereignty that Unit 4 takes up.

Tokenisation, in short, changes the plumbing of finance. It does not change the promise: who owes what to whom, under which law, and backed by which money. A well-designed tokenised product starts with the promise and only then chooses the pipes.

Design the legal claim and the cash leg first; the token is the last decision, not the first.
