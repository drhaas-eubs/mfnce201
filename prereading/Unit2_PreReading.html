Key framework
Do You Need a Blockchain?

A ledger without a keeper

For most of financial history, records of ownership have had a keeper. A bank keeps the record of your deposit, a central securities depository the record of your shares, a land registry the record of your house. We trust the record because we trust the keeper — and because the keeper is supervised, audited and liable when things go wrong.

In 2008 a pseudonymous author, Satoshi Nakamoto, published a nine-page paper proposing a system in which no keeper was needed. Bitcoin's ledger is copied across thousands of computers. Transactions are grouped into blocks, and each block contains the cryptographic hash — a unique digital fingerprint — of the block before it. Change one past transaction and every later fingerprint changes, so tampering is visible to everyone. Users prove ownership not by showing an ID to a clerk but by signing transactions with a private key, which anyone can verify with the matching public key, a technique that goes back to Diffie and Hellman's work in 1976.

The hard part is agreement

Copying a ledger is easy. Agreeing on which copy is correct, when some participants may be dishonest, is hard. Computer scientists had formalised this as the Byzantine Generals Problem in 1982: how can generals coordinate an attack when some of them may be traitors sending contradictory messages? Lamport and his co-authors showed that agreement is possible only if fewer than one third of participants are faulty.

Bitcoin's answer is proof of work. Anyone may propose the next block, but only after solving a computational puzzle that costs real electricity. Cheating would require out-spending the honest majority. Proof of stake, which Ethereum adopted in 2022, replaces electricity with capital: validators lock up tokens that they lose if they misbehave. Permissioned blockchains used by banks often rely on older voting protocols among a known set of validators, which are faster and final but only as trustworthy as the members. Each mechanism is a different trade-off between openness, speed, cost and finality.

Ethereum added a second idea: programs on the ledger. Smart contracts, a concept described by Nick Szabo in 1997, are code that executes automatically when conditions are met. They allow tokens, exchanges and lending markets to run on a blockchain without an operator. They also execute bugs with the same reliability as intentions, and they cannot know anything about the outside world unless someone — an oracle — tells them.

Ask the questions before the technology

Between 2016 and 2022 banks, insurers and logistics firms launched dozens of blockchain consortia. Many have since closed, including several trade-finance networks. Their technology often worked. What failed was the business case: too few users, too much coordination, and problems that a well-run shared database could have solved more cheaply.

Karl Wüst and Arthur Gervais turned this lesson into a decision tree. Do you need to store state? Are there multiple writers? Is there a trusted third party that is always online? Are all writers known? Do they trust each other? If a trusted third party can do the job, use it. If the writers are known and trust each other, use a shared database. Only if writers are known but do not trust each other does a permissioned blockchain make sense; only if they are unknown does a public blockchain. Every 'yes' to trust is a reason not to use a blockchain.

Marco Iansiti and Karim Lakhani add a time dimension. Blockchain, they argue, is a foundational technology like TCP/IP, not a disruptive one. Its adoption will start with single-use and local applications and reach transformation of whole markets only after decades of coordination among firms, regulators and users.

Public, consortium and private ledgers

Not all blockchains are open to everyone. Daniel Drescher's distinction between who may read a ledger and who may write to it gives four combinations. Bitcoin and Ethereum are public and permissionless: anyone can read every transaction and anyone with the right hardware or stake can help validate. At the other extreme, a private permissioned ledger is run by one organisation for its own purposes, which makes it very close to a conventional database with extra cryptographic guarantees. Between them sit consortium chains, in which a defined group of institutions — for example banks — operate validator nodes and agree on the rules together.

Financial institutions have mostly chosen the consortium or private model. It gives them known counterparties, control over who sees which data, faster finality and an easier fit with regulation. The price is that the ledger is only as trustworthy as its members and their governance. If the same few banks that already run a market also run its blockchain, the question becomes what the technology adds that a jointly owned database would not. Public chains, by contrast, offer neutrality and open access but force regulated firms to work with validators they do not know, data that everyone can read, and transaction fees that rise when the network is busy.

The choice of platform is therefore a choice of trust model, not merely a technical preference. When you meet a blockchain project in practice, the first questions to ask are: who runs the nodes, who can see the data, who can change the rules, and what happens if a member leaves?

What this means for finance

In securities markets the relevant question is precise: does a blockchain remove a trusted party that is costly, slow or risky, or does it simply add a new one in a different place? The answer differs for a Bitcoin payment, a bank consortium and a tokenised bond. Unit 3 applies this question to tokenisation and decentralised finance. The test you learn in this unit — ask about trust before you ask about technology — will be needed every time.

Every 'yes' to the question 'can we trust someone?' is a reason not to build a blockchain.
