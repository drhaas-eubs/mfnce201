Lessig's Four Modalities

The efficiency promise

Advocates of blockchain in finance make three main promises. Markets will be more efficient, because settlement is faster and intermediaries are fewer. Markets will be more transparent, because transactions are recorded on a shared ledger that all participants can inspect. And new opportunities will open up — tokenised assets, programmable money, faster cross-border payments — that today's infrastructure cannot support.

Eugene Fama's efficient market hypothesis offers a benchmark. In an efficient market, prices reflect available information. A public blockchain makes certain information — every transaction, every balance at every address — available to everyone instantly. That might suggest more efficient prices. But transparency also creates new problems. On public chains, pending transactions are visible before they are confirmed, allowing others to trade ahead of them. Transparency of transactions is not the same as transparency of who is behind them, nor of what the code will do in a situation its authors did not foresee.

Code is law — and why that is a warning

In 1999 the legal scholar Lawrence Lessig argued that behaviour is regulated by four forces: law, social norms, markets and architecture. In cyberspace, architecture means code. Software can permit or forbid an action before any court is involved. Lessig's phrase 'code is law' is often quoted by blockchain enthusiasts as a slogan. He meant it as a warning: whoever writes the code exercises regulatory power, often without the accountability that applies to legislators.

Blockchain finance shows both sides. A smart contract can enforce collateral rules more consistently than any human. But when a contract contains a bug, it executes the bug just as reliably. When the private keys to a token contract are held by two employees without proper safeguards, the most transparent ledger in the world will faithfully record the theft. Accountability — the ability to identify who is responsible and to obtain redress — requires the other three modalities: law that assigns liability, norms that shape professional conduct, and markets that reward reliability.

Regulators catch up

Regulators have responded with a principle: same activity, same risk, same regulation. The Financial Stability Board adopted it in its 2023 recommendations. The EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation applies it by sorting crypto-assets into e-money tokens, asset-referenced tokens and other crypto-assets, each with authorisation, reserve and disclosure requirements. Tokenised financial instruments remain under securities law and, for market infrastructures using distributed ledgers, the DLT Pilot Regime.

Cybersecurity is a central part of this regulatory catch-up. Frameworks such as the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, which added 'Govern' to its five earlier functions in 2024, apply to blockchain systems as much as to any other. The difference is that blockchain's immutability makes recovery harder: a fraudulent transaction cannot simply be reversed.

Engineering limits and their consequences

Some implications follow from design constraints rather than from regulation. Vitalik Buterin has described a trilemma: a blockchain can easily achieve only two of scalability, security and decentralisation. Public chains that maximise decentralisation and security process far fewer transactions per second than card networks or securities settlement systems. Permissioned chains are faster because they rely on a small number of known validators — which is to say, they trade decentralisation for speed. Layer-2 networks and sharding try to stretch the triangle, at the price of added complexity.

The trilemma mirrors the Impossible Trinity from Unit 1. Just as a country must choose two of a fixed exchange rate, free capital and independent monetary policy, a ledger designer must choose where to compromise. Recognising the trade-off protects decision-makers from claims that a new platform offers everything at once. It also clarifies an ethical point: whoever decides which property to sacrifice also decides who bears the resulting risk — the users of a slower system, the participants excluded from a permissioned one, or the customers exposed to a less secure one.

Ethics and society

Beyond regulation lie broader questions. Who gains from lower remittance costs, and who loses — the incumbent banks, or the workers in their payment departments? What happens to people who lose their keys or cannot use an app? How much energy does a consensus mechanism use, and who bears the environmental cost? What happens to a country's monetary sovereignty when payments move to tokens issued in another currency? Value-sensitive design, developed by Batya Friedman, asks designers to identify the values at stake and all affected stakeholders — including indirect ones — before choosing a technical solution. John Elkington's triple bottom line asks organisations to account for people and planet as well as profit.

The future is unlikely to be either the replacement of banks by blockchains or the disappearance of blockchain from finance. The Bank for International Settlements has proposed a 'unified ledger' on which tokenised central bank money, bank deposits and assets coexist. Whether this or another model prevails, the questions in this unit will remain: who writes the rules, who is answerable when they fail, and who shares in the gains.

A ledger can show what happened; only law, norms and markets can decide who answers for it.
