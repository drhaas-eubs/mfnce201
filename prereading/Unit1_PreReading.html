Key framework
Trade Lifecycle

The screen is the smallest part of the market

When a student opens Yahoo Finance and sees a share price tick upward, it is natural to think of the market as that number. Prices are what the media report and what investors watch. Yet the price is only the visible tip of a much larger arrangement. Behind every trade lies a sequence of steps that transforms an agreement to exchange into an actual change of ownership. Finance professionals call this sequence the trade lifecycle: pre-trade checks, execution, confirmation, clearing, settlement and custody.

Execution — the moment buyer and seller agree on price and quantity — now takes microseconds on electronic venues. Everything that follows takes longer. In the European Union, most securities trades still settle two business days after the trade date (T+2). Until settlement, the buyer has a contractual claim, not the security, and the seller has a promise of cash, not the cash. The market's real work happens in this gap.

Three institutions that turn promises into property

Three kinds of institution make the gap safe. Exchanges and other trading venues bring buyers and sellers together and set prices; their quality shows up in liquidity and in the size of the bid-ask spread. Central counterparties (CCPs) step into the middle of trades through novation, becoming buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer. They net obligations across all members, so that thousands of trades reduce to a few payments, and they collect margin so that a member's default does not spread. Central securities depositories (CSDs) hold the definitive record of who owns which securities and move them against payment.

The principle that binds the last step is delivery versus payment (DvP). Securities move if and only if cash moves. Without DvP, a seller could deliver and never be paid. The Bank for International Settlements described three DvP models in 1992; they differ in whether securities and cash are settled trade by trade or in net batches, but all remove the risk of losing the full principal of a trade.

Regulators sit above all of this. The Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures, published by the Committee on Payment and Settlement Systems and IOSCO in 2012, set global standards for CCPs, CSDs and payment systems. In the EU, the Central Securities Depositories Regulation sets settlement rules and penalties for failed trades.

Why the industry is shortening the gap

Every hour between trade and settlement is an hour of counterparty risk and an hour in which collateral is tied up. The United States moved to T+1 in May 2024. The EU, the UK and Switzerland will follow on 11 October 2027, under an amendment to the EU's Central Securities Depositories Regulation. Shorter cycles reduce risk and margin, but they compress the time available to confirm trades, arrange financing and, for foreign investors, buy the currency needed to pay. That last point links securities markets directly to the foreign exchange market.

A US fund that buys a euro-denominated bond must buy euros. Under T+1 it must do so on the trade date, often in a different time zone. The foreign exchange market, the largest financial market in the world by turnover, therefore becomes part of the securities trade lifecycle. Exchange rates are driven over the long run by relative inflation (purchasing power parity) and over shorter horizons by interest differentials and expectations (interest rate parity and the international Fisher effect). For a company that earns in one currency and spends in another, these forces determine how much of its profit survives conversion.

Who takes part, and why it matters

Markets are also populations. On the buy side sit pension funds, insurers, asset managers, hedge funds and, increasingly, retail investors trading through apps. On the sell side sit brokers who execute orders, dealers and market makers who quote prices from their own inventory, and investment banks that bring new securities to market. Between them stand the infrastructure providers — venues, CCPs, CSDs and custodian banks — and above them the supervisors: in Europe, ESMA and national authorities such as BaFin, together with central banks that oversee payment and settlement systems.

Each group earns money from a different part of the chain and carries a different risk. A market maker profits from the bid-ask spread but must hold inventory when prices move; Hans Stoll showed that part of every spread compensates dealers for the risk of trading with better-informed counterparties. A custodian earns fees for safekeeping and must never lose a client's assets. A CCP earns clearing fees and must survive the default of its largest member. When you assess any proposal to redesign the market — including one built on blockchain — ask which of these participants it removes, which it keeps, and whether the risk they carried disappears or simply moves somewhere else.

Currency markets add a further layer. Unlike shares, currencies trade mainly over the counter between banks, with no single exchange or CCP for most spot trades. Settlement of the two currency legs in different time zones creates its own risk, historically known as Herstatt risk after a German bank whose failure in 1974 left counterparties paid in one currency but not the other. Industry utilities now settle many FX trades payment-versus-payment, a principle you will meet again in Unit 3.

What this means for blockchain

Blockchain enters this course as a challenge to the settlement gap. Its promise is that a shared, tamper-evident ledger could make execution and settlement nearly simultaneous, with securities and cash moving atomically in one transaction. Whether that promise is realistic depends on understanding exactly what today's institutions do, what risks they remove, and what they cost. Students who can explain why a CCP nets trades, why DvP exists and why T+1 is hard will be able to judge which parts of the chain a blockchain could replace and which it would simply have to rebuild. That is the analytical habit this unit is designed to build.

Before asking what blockchain can do for markets, know what clearing, settlement and custody already do — and what they cost.
